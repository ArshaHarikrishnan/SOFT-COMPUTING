Module 5 – Multi-Objective Optimization & Hybrid System

Teaching plan: 8 hours | Course Outcome: CO5

Syllabus/question-bank coverage: MOOP; linear/nonlinear and convex/non-convex problems; objectives and Pareto principles; dominance, Pareto optimality and dominance-relation properties; non-dominated set; optimality conditions; neuro-fuzzy and genetic-neuro hybrid systems.

1. Multi-Objective Optimization Problem (MOOP)

A MOOP involves optimizing two or more objective functions simultaneously, subject to decision-variable constraints. Objectives may conflict, so one solution may not be best for every objective.

Minimise / Maximise F(x) = [f₁(x), f₂(x), …, fₖ(x)]ᵀ
Subject to: gᵢ(x) ≤ 0, i = 1,…,m; hⱼ(x) = 0, j = 1,…,p
x ∈ X (feasible decision space)

Example: Design a vehicle to minimise cost and fuel consumption while satisfying safety and performance constraints.

2. Classification of MOOP

ClassificationMeaning
Linear MOOPObjective functions and constraints are linear in the decision variables.
Nonlinear MOOPAt least one objective function or constraint is nonlinear.
Convex MOOPFor a minimisation formulation, the objective functions are convex and the feasible region is convex (with suitable convex constraints).
Non-convex MOOPThe feasible region or relevant objective structure is non-convex; the Pareto front may contain non-convex portions or disconnected regions.
Do not confuse linear/nonlinear with convex/non-convex. These describe different properties: linearity concerns mathematical form, while convexity concerns the shape/properties of functions and feasible sets.

3. Principles of Multi-Objective Optimization

  • Identify decision variables, objective functions and constraints.
  • Recognise trade-offs among conflicting objectives.
  • Compare candidate solutions using a dominance relation.
  • Retain non-dominated solutions rather than assuming one candidate is best in every objective.
  • Present the set of trade-off solutions to support final decision-making.

4. Dominance Relations

For a minimisation problem, compare two feasible solutions A and B across all objectives.

  • Weak dominance: A is no worse than B in every objective (all objective values are less than or equal to B's), with equality permitted.
  • Strict/Pareto dominance: A is no worse than B in every objective and strictly better in at least one objective.
  • Non-dominance: neither solution dominates the other; each may be better in a different objective.
For minimisation: A dominates B if fᵢ(A) ≤ fᵢ(B) for all i,
and fⱼ(A) < fⱼ(B) for at least one objective j.

For maximisation, reverse the inequalities. Always state whether the problem is minimisation or maximisation before comparing solutions.

5. Pareto Optimality and Pareto Front

  • A feasible solution is Pareto-optimal if no other feasible solution dominates it.
  • The Pareto-optimal set is the collection of Pareto-optimal decision solutions.
  • The Pareto front is the image of that set in objective space.
  • Different points on the front represent different trade-offs among objectives.
Important: Pareto optimality does not mean that every objective has reached its individual minimum. It means that improving one objective is not possible without worsening at least one other objective, within the feasible set.

6. Identifying a Non-Dominated Set

  1. List all feasible candidate solutions and their objective values.
  2. Choose the optimisation direction for every objective (minimise or maximise).
  3. Compare each candidate against the others, objective by objective.
  4. Mark a candidate as dominated if another candidate is at least as good in every objective and strictly better in at least one (for minimisation, reverse signs for maximisation).
  5. Remove dominated candidates. The remaining candidates form the non-dominated set among the candidates examined.
  6. Plot their objective values to visualise the Pareto front when there are two objectives.

7. Properties of a Dominance Relation

State properties with the exact dominance relation and convention being used. In the standard Pareto comparison, strict dominance is irreflexive and transitive; it is asymmetric. The corresponding weak componentwise relation is reflexive and transitive. Antisymmetry applies to the weak componentwise relation on objective vectors: if each vector is no worse than the other in every component, their objective vectors are equal.

8. Optimality Conditions in MOO

Optimality is assessed against all objectives and the feasible region, not by optimising one objective in isolation. A feasible point is Pareto-optimal when no feasible alternative improves at least one objective without worsening any other. In differentiable continuous problems, necessary conditions may be expressed using weighted combinations of objective gradients together with constraint conditions; the exact conditions depend on convexity, differentiability and constraint qualifications.

9. Neuro-Fuzzy Hybrid Systems

A neuro-fuzzy system combines neural-network learning/adaptation with fuzzy logic's linguistic rules and approximate reasoning.

  • Fuzzy logic represents knowledge using linguistic variables, membership functions and IF–THEN rules.
  • Neural-network learning adjusts parameters such as membership-function shapes or rule-related parameters using data.
  • The hybrid approach can combine interpretability of fuzzy rules with data-driven adaptation.

Common classification by learning architecture:

  • Cooperative: neural network assists in developing or tuning the fuzzy system; after learning, the network may no longer be part of the final inference system.
  • Concurrent: neural network and fuzzy system operate together, with the network adapting or supporting the fuzzy system during operation.
  • Integrated: neural learning mechanisms are embedded in the fuzzy system architecture, forming a unified neuro-fuzzy model (for example, ANFIS-style architectures).

10. Genetic-Neuro Hybrid Systems

A genetic-neuro hybrid system combines a Genetic Algorithm (GA) with a Neural Network (NN). The GA performs population-based search/optimisation, while the neural network learns patterns or models the problem.

Problem / Data → Neural Network model → Fitness evaluation
Population of candidate network parameters / structures → Genetic Algorithm
Selection → Crossover → Mutation → Candidate networks → Evaluation → Best network
  • GA can optimise neural-network weights, architecture, learning parameters or feature selections.
  • NN can provide prediction or classification capability and evaluate candidate solutions.
  • The GA explores alternatives and may help avoid dependence on a single gradient-based search path.

Advantages: global search capability, flexible optimisation of network parameters/structure, reduced dependence on gradient information for the GA search, and potential improvement in network performance. The method may require substantial computation.

11. Neuro-Fuzzy vs Genetic-Neuro

FeatureNeuro-fuzzyGenetic-neuro
CombinationNeural learning + fuzzy reasoningGenetic optimisation + neural network
Main role of learning/searchAdapt fuzzy membership functions/rules or system parametersSearch for suitable network weights, structures or parameters
Knowledge representationFuzzy sets and linguistic IF–THEN rulesNeural-network architecture and parameters
Typical focusInterpretable approximate reasoning with adaptationOptimising neural-network performance or structure
